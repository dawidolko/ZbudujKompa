Power supply calculator

Enter the power draw of your CPU and graphics card. The result includes the headroom a supply actually needs.

Use the peak figure: AMD PPT, or Intel PL2

Board, memory, drives and fans

Printed on the box; anything bought since 2023 is usually ATX 3.x

Recommended supply

650W

Estimated draw460 W
Transient peak~610 W
Load at the recommended size71%
The recommendation adds 30 per cent on top of the estimated draw. That margin is not spare capacity for more parts — it keeps the supply near its efficiency peak and absorbs the brief spikes modern graphics cards produce. Those spikes are why the standard matters: an ATX 3.x supply is required to ride out 180 per cent of its rated power for a millisecond, so the ordinary margin suffices. An older supply is not, and its protection trips on the spike rather than the average — which is why the recommendation rises when you select one.

Will it fit

Case specifications list card length and cooler height separately, so parts that each fit can still fail together.

From the case specification

Verdict

Graphics cardCPU cooler
Card margin+26 mm
Cooler margin+10 mm
The figures a case publishes are measured independently of each other. A front-mounted radiator sits where the card would go, so it takes its own thickness plus its fans — around 55 mm — out of the length available. This is the most common way a build fails to assemble despite every individual part being within spec. Leave a little margin beyond zero: power cables need somewhere to go behind the card.

The cost is calculated from the draw at the wall, which is the DC figure divided by the supply efficiency — a 400 W build on a Gold unit is billed as 444 W. Most calculators skip that step and understate the bill by about a tenth. Idle and load are separated because a gaming machine idles for most of its life, and costing every hour at peak draw overstates it several times over.

Fan noise

What several fans actually add up to. The answer is not what most people expect.

From the datasheet; typically 20–35 dBA

Leave at zero for an air-cooled build

Together

32.8dBA

Comparable toa library
Versus a single fan+4.8 dBA
If you doubled the count35.8 dBA (+3.0)
Perceived loudness1.39× as loud
Decibels are logarithmic, so they cannot be added: two identical 30 dBA fans give 33 dBA, not 60. Doubling the number of fans always adds exactly 3 dB, which is heard as roughly 1.2× as loud rather than twice. The figure is an upper bound — a case absorbs some of it — and it assumes the datasheet levels were measured at the same distance, which between manufacturers they often are not.

Fan speed and noise

The largest single lever for a quiet machine, and it is counter-intuitive.

What your fan curve actually runs at

Roughly the total power draw of the build

Noise at 70% speed

22.3dBA

Quieter by−7.7 dBA
Airflow42.0 CFM
Airflow lost−30%
Power used34%
Air temperature rise+5.6 °C
Airflow falls in proportion to speed, but noise falls with the fifth power of it. Dropping to 70 per cent costs 30 per cent of the airflow and buys about 8 dB, which is clearly audible where the airflow loss usually is not. The relationship breaks down below roughly 500 rpm, where motor and bearing noise stop scaling. Rated airflow is measured with no restriction at all, so real flow through filters and a radiator is well below it.

Monitors are sold by diagonal, but desk space is limited by width, and the two diverge sharply between 16:9 and ultrawide. The distance figure is where a pixel subtends one arcminute — the standard figure for 20/20 vision — so it is a threshold for a typical eye rather than a recommendation. Manufacturers also round diagonals, which introduces more error than anything else here.

Whether your cable carries the mode your monitor advertises.

The comparison is against the payload rate rather than the headline signalling rate, and the gap between the two decides the outcome: 4K at 144 Hz needs compression on DisplayPort 1.4 but fits HDMI 2.1 uncompressed. DSC is described by VESA as visually lossless, which is a claim validated by subjective testing rather than a mathematical guarantee. Note also that a device labelled HDMI 2.1 may negotiate a lower rate than the full 48 Gbps.

Memory latency

What a CAS number actually means in nanoseconds, and why a higher one is not automatically slower.

MT/s, not MHz — DDR transfers on both clock edges

Two modules populate both channels

First word

10.00ns

Verdictgood
Theoretical bandwidth96.0 GB/s
With one module only48.0 GB/s (−50%)
Versus DDR5-6000 CL300.00 ns faster
CAS latency counts clock cycles, so it only becomes comparable once converted to time. DDR5-6000 CL30 and DDR4-3200 CL16 both arrive in 10 ns — the DDR5 kit has a higher CAS number and a proportionally faster clock. This is the best case, where the row is already open; a random access can cost three times as much. Bandwidth is a theoretical peak, and real throughput runs 60–85 per cent of it.

The missing capacity is arithmetic, not a defect: manufacturers count a gigabyte as a thousand million bytes, while the operating system divides by 1024 three times. The seven per cent difference is the same on every drive from every brand. Endurance is included mainly to show the worry is misplaced — at ordinary desktop write rates a modern drive outlasts the machine several times over.

Temperature under load

What the processor will sit at, split into what the case adds and what the cooler adds.

Use the peak figure: AMD PPT or Intel PL2

The two contributions are shown separately because they call for different fixes: a high case figure means more or better-placed fans, while a high cooler figure means a bigger cooler. The case rise is a tiered estimate rather than a computation, since real airflow cannot be measured from the outside. Reaching 95 °C is not a fault on modern processors — boost algorithms treat it as a target — but it does mean the cooler is what limits performance.

Frames and refresh rate

What your monitor can actually show, and what happens to the rest.

Frames shown

140fps

well matched
Panel utilisation97%
Beyond the refresh rate0 fps

Frames rendered above the refresh rate are not entirely wasted — the frame being scanned out is more recent, which lowers latency slightly — but they are not seen. The reverse case matters more: a 240 Hz panel fed 90 frames is mostly unused, and the money would have gone further on the card. Note also that the returns diminish sharply: 60 to 120 frames removes 8.3 ms of frame time, while 240 to 360 removes 1.4 ms.

M.2 slot speed

Whether the slot you put a drive in limits it — an invisible mistake, since nothing warns you.

Slot allows

7.88 GB/s

the slot limits the drive
Drive is capable of15.75 GB/s
Performance gain−50%
The second and third M.2 slots on a board frequently run at a lower PCIe generation or on fewer lanes than the first, and nothing tells you when a drive is being held back. The practical rule is to put the fastest drive in the slot nearest the processor, which is the one wired directly to it. Bear in mind that sequential speed barely affects game loading anyway — random reads dominate there, and those are similar across generations.

Is an upgrade worth it

How much performance a change buys, and whether the difference is one you would actually notice.

+106%

a noticeable difference
Approximate cost~4050 zł
Points per 100 zł0.89 pkt
Below roughly 15 per cent, a difference is rarely perceptible while playing — it shows on a chart and not in use. The cost used is the new part on its own rather than the difference between the two, because the old one only offsets it if you actually sell it. Points per hundred złoty exist for comparison between options; the absolute figure means nothing on its own.

Balance check

Which component limits a pairing — as a direction, not a false precision.

At 1080p

Processor limits it

about 34% apart
1080pCPU (34%)
1440pCPU (12%)
4KGPU (16%)
This shows a direction, not a measurement. Bottleneck percentages imply a precision that does not exist — the real answer depends on the game, the settings and the scene. What is reliable is the pattern: raising the resolution shifts load onto the graphics card, so the same pairing is progressively less processor-limited as resolution rises. A pairing that looks unbalanced at 1080p is often fine at 4K.
